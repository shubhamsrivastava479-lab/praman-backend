Resumes are written to impress. Dates are stretched, gaps are hidden, and a three-month job can become a year. Employment history is among the most common areas where discrepancies appear in background checks, so HR teams look for a record that does not depend on the candidate's word.

## What EPFO records can tell you

The Employees' Provident Fund Organisation keeps provident fund records for employees of covered establishments. Each member has a Universal Account Number (UAN). When an employer deposits PF, the member's history builds up. Used with consent, this history can show:

- the **employers** a person worked for
- **joining and leaving dates**
- how **continuous** the work history is

This is useful because it comes from a source other than the candidate or a reference they choose.

## What it cannot tell you

- It covers only people whose employers deposited PF. Freelancers, gig workers, informal workers and some small establishments may not appear.
- It does not show the designation, the salary, or why someone left.
- A missing record is not proof of lying. It simply means you need another way to check.

## How HR teams use it well

1. **Take consent first.** Explain what you are checking and why.
2. **Compare with the resume.** Do the employers and dates broadly match?
3. **Investigate gaps and mismatches calmly.** Ask the candidate. There are many honest explanations, such as a name change of the company or a delay in PF deposits.
4. **Add another source when needed:** a salary slip, relieving letter or a call to the employer's HR.
5. **Record the outcome** with the candidate's file.

## Where candidates slip up

Common mismatches include an inflated tenure, an employer who is not in the history at all, or overlapping jobs. A mismatch is a reason to ask a question, not to reject someone straight away.
