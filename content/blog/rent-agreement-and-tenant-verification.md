Many landlords believe a signed rent agreement is enough protection. It is not. The agreement records what both sides promise. It does not tell you whether the person signing is real, employed or trustworthy. Verification and the agreement do different jobs, and you need both.

## Why it matters

If a tenant stops paying, damages the property or uses it for something else, you will rely on the agreement and on being able to find the person. A well-written agreement with a false identity behind it is worth very little.

## What the agreement does

- Sets rent, deposit, due dates and increase terms
- Defines who may live in the property and for what use
- Explains maintenance, repairs and utilities
- Sets the notice period and exit conditions
- Records the condition of the property at move-in

## What verification does

- **Confirms identity:** the person matches a government photo ID.
- **Confirms address and contact:** where they lived and how to reach them.
- **Checks work or study:** a real employer or college.
- **Checks references:** a previous landlord or employer.
- **Supports police rules:** where intimation or verification is required.

## How to do both well

1. **Verify first,** then draft the agreement using the names on the ID.
2. **Include every adult occupant** in the details.
3. **State the use** of the property clearly.
4. **Register or stamp** the agreement as local rules require.
5. **Keep copies** of ID and the agreement, stored securely.
6. **Take photos** of the property at move-in and move-out.
7. **Inform the police** if your city requires it.

## Common mistakes

- Signing a template without reading the terms
- Using a different name in the agreement and on the bank account
- Accepting cash with no receipt
- Skipping verification because the tenant seems nice
