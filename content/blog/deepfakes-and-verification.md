Deepfakes, images, videos and voices created or altered by software, are now cheap and convincing. For businesses, the risk is not only fake celebrity clips. It is a cloned voice asking finance to pay an invoice, an edited face on a KYC video or a fake candidate in an interview.

## Why it matters

Verification used to assume that seeing and hearing someone meant it was them. That assumption is weaker now. Businesses that rely on a single selfie or a single call can be fooled.

## Where businesses see it

- **Voice cloning:** a call from a 'senior' asking for urgent payment.
- **Video KYC:** edited video or a replayed recording.
- **Hiring:** altered faces or voices in remote interviews.
- **Customer support:** impersonating customers to reset accounts.
- **Claims:** edited photos and documents.

## How to reduce the risk

1. **Use layered checks,** not just one: ID, face match, liveness and device or location signals.
2. **Use live prompts,** such as turning the head or reading a random number, which are harder to fake in advance.
3. **Confirm sensitive requests on a second channel,** for example a call back on a known number.
4. **Set payment rules,** such as two approvers and a cooling period for new accounts.
5. **Train staff** to pause when a request is urgent and unusual.
6. **Keep a human reviewer** for flagged cases.
7. **Record and log checks** so you can investigate later.

## What not to do

- Do not rely on a single photo or recording
- Do not treat a match score as the final answer
- Do not shame staff for double-checking

## Plan for the day it happens

Decide in advance who to call, how to freeze payments and how to preserve evidence if a deepfake attempt succeeds. Report the incident to your bank and to the cybercrime helpline 1930 quickly, because speed often decides whether money can be recovered.
