Families increasingly verify a match before committing to marriage. That is sensible. But how you verify matters as much as what you verify. Checks done with consent build trust. Checks done in secret damage it, and can cause legal trouble.

## Why consent matters

- **Respect:** the person is a partner, not a suspect.
- **Law and privacy:** collecting private data without consent can break privacy rules.
- **Accuracy:** people share more honestly when they feel respected.
- **Relationship:** discovering a secret check later can hurt trust before the marriage begins.
- **Mutual benefit:** both sides can ask for the same checks.

## What consent looks like

1. **Explain the purpose** of the check and what it covers.
2. **Ask clearly,** in writing or on a recorded call.
3. **Let the person share documents themselves.**
4. **Limit the scope** to what is relevant: identity, marital status, job, education, family and any serious concerns.
5. **Keep it mutual** so both sides are treated the same.
6. **Protect the data** and delete it when the process ends.
7. **Respect a refusal** and discuss what it means, instead of investigating secretly.

## What to avoid

- Hiring someone to follow or spy on a person
- Collecting medical or financial information without permission
- Sharing results widely with relatives and friends
- Using verification to pressure or shame

## If a check raises a concern

Talk about it directly and calmly first. Ask for an explanation and supporting documents. Many concerns come from misunderstandings, so give the person a chance to respond.

## Building trust

A verification done openly often brings families closer, because both sides see that the other is serious and honest. Treat it as the start of a respectful relationship, not as a test one side must pass.
